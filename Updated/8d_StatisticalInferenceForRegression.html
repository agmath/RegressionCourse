Statistical Learning Reminders

In the statistical learning context, we come with a hypothesized model form:

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + \varepsilon\]

or, equivalently…

\[\mathbb{E}\left[y\right] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k\]

Proposing such a model comes with several questions – one global (about the model as a whole), and potentially many local (about individual model terms).

Global Test for Model Utility

Intuitive Version: Does my model have any explanatory value at all?

Formal Hypotheses: \(\begin{array}{lcl} H_{0} & : & \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_k = 0\\ H_{a} & : & \text{At least one explanatory variable has a}\\ & & \text{non-zero coefficient}\end{array}\)

Local, Term-Based Tests

Intuitive Version: Does this particular model term provide explanatory value, given all of the other model terms?

Formal Hypotheses:
\(\begin{array}{lcl} H_0 & : & \beta_{~i} = 0\\ H_a & : & \beta_{~i} \neq 0\end{array}\)

The output from glance()ing at our fitted model object gives lots of additional information about overall model fit and quality.

  • The R-squared value (r.squared) measures the proportion of variation in the response variable which is explained by the terms in our fitted model.

    • “About 60.45% of the variation in y is explained by linear relationships with x1 and x2 (our model).”

  • The adjusted R-squared value (adj.r.squared) is a better measure for comparing models than R-squared because adjusted R-squared penalizes the inclusion of additional model terms, while R-squared never decreases with additional terms.

  • The sigma value is our residual standard error, which is roughly the typical size of a residual (prediction error). It is often referred to as a biased measure because it is too optimistic (estimated from the data the model was trained on).

    • Considering the data our model was trained on, we expect a typical prediction error to be about 188.6, but on new or previously unseen observations we should expect that error to be higher.

  • We won’t generally consider the additional items in our course.

Confidence Intervals (Model Coefficients)

Whenever we estimate a parameter using data, there is uncertainty associated with that estimate.

The \(\beta\)-coefficients we estimate during model fitting are parameters, and there is uncertainty associated with their values.

Each fitted coefficient is accompanied by a standard error estimate (std.error), which can be used to construct a confidence interval.

As a reminder, an approximate 95% confidence interval can be constructed using:

\[\left(\substack{\text{Point}\\\text{Estimate}}\right) \pm 2\cdot \left(\substack{\text{Standard}\\\text{Error}}\right)\]

Model Predictions

When we build a model, we often want to use it to make predictions on new sets of observations (counterfactuals/hypotheticals or future occurrences).

They’re all wrong!

Solution: We’ll have two types of intervals associated with model predictions, depending on our goals. We can construct confidence intervals to capture an average response, or prediction intervals to capture an individual response (each with some degree of confidence).

Confidence Intervals (Predictions)

The formula for confidence intervals on predictions is complex!

\[\displaystyle{\left(\tt{point~estimate}\right)\pm t^*_{\text{df}}\cdot \left(\tt{RMSE}\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_{new} - \bar{x})^2}{\sum{\left(x - \bar{x}\right)^2}}}\right)}\]

We’ll use R to construct these intervals for us.

Prediction Intervals

Confidence intervals are useful for capturing the average response given values of the explanatory/predictor variables.

Often times though, we are more interested in predicting an individual response – prediction intervals will do that.

There’s more uncertainty associated with predicting an individual response than an average response though, so these prediction intervals will be wider than the corresponding confidence intervals.

Summary

Below are the most common applications of statistical inference in regression modeling.

  • Hypothesis Tests

    • Global Test for Model Utility: Does our model have any utility at all?
    • Individual Term-Based Tests: Are each of the included predictor terms useful?

  • Confidence Intervals

    • What is the plausible range for each parameter/coefficient?

      • How do we interpret those ranges?

    • Can we make reliable predictions?

      • Confidence Intervals for average response
      • Prediction Intervals for individual response

We’ll be utilizing all of these ideas throughout our course.

We’ll leverage R functionality to obtain intervals or to calculate test statistics and \(p\)-values though, since it is much faster and more accurate than doing any of this by hand.
